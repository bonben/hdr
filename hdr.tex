\chapter{Foreword}
The common thread in my work is the simplification of algorithms and efficient implementation, which leads to the principle: you can do just as well with less. The richness of the work primarily comes from the interaction between experts in specific algorithmic fields, mainly channel coding and deep learning, with someone who is concerned with computational and memory economy.

This is what truly motivates me in my research. There is a real pleasure in discovering a new problem and its solutions, and then in a second phase, reflecting with a partner on what can be removed, simplified, factored, and streamlined. It's a celebration of sobriety. One could, lacking humility, claim a consistently positive impact from the perspective of the energy consumption of digital systems and their overall carbon footprint. However, the savings achieved are actually used to perform more processing and to deploy them wherever possible. These issues are more economic and political in nature than scientific.

What motivates me more is the intrinsic challenge of each problem. How far can we go? What is the real impact of the choices we make? I am very fond of competitions in the academic environment, whether in a research or teaching context. Not for the competition itself (though, I must admit, it's in my nature), but mainly because it allows for the definition of an objective criterion and clear procedures to fairly compare implementations.

During this past decade spent in the research world, it is tiring to see published works that are inapplicable but well-marketed. Confronting research ideas with an objective criterion helps to avoid this kind of drift. I have developed competitive aspects in two courses I teach at IMT Atlantique, which I detail in the Appendix~\ref{sec:spotlight_courses}. Competition can also consist of choosing a set of constraints to restrict a more complex problem and then optimizing a metric. This is what I did in my thesis on the implementation of error-correcting codes on general-purpose processors. It is what I pushed Hugo Tessier to do in his thesis on neural network pruning. It is also what I proposed to Hugo Le Blévec in his thesis on the implementation of neural networks for semantic segmentation on FPGAs. Or with a group of students on the Open Hardware competition organized by AMD/Xilinx. Finally, during the implementation competition at the ISTC 2025 conference.

These works and experiences consistently allow for the acquisition of experience and certainties about the methods that do or do not have a strong impact on the performance and efficiency of systems. It is an empirical knowledge that is built over the course of projects and collaborations, and which industrialists are very keen on.

However, while it is necessary to compare ourselves on objective, measurable criteria within controlled frameworks, I am of the opinion that my research activities have focused a little too much on this type of contribution, to the detriment of a more long-term vision aimed at a positive impact on society. The objective of this document is, after summarizing my past activities, to propose perspectives that allow for the application of the knowledge acquired toward a more ambitious goal than winning a competition or being state-of-the-art on a benchmark.

\chapter{Digital Communication Algorithms on Programmable Architectures}
\label{sec:chaines_communication}


Software implementations of error-correcting code decoders play a crucial role in several areas of modern digital communications. Firstly, they are essential for Monte Carlo simulations, enabling the evaluation of communication system performance under various noise and interference scenarios. Secondly, in the context of Cloud-RAN (Cloud Radio Access Networks), software decoders offer the flexibility needed to virtualize physical layer functions, thereby facilitating cooperative multi-point processing. These decoders are indeed the most computationally intensive building blocks of the communication chain. Finally, for software-defined radios (SDR), they allow dynamic reconfiguration of communication parameters, adapting systems to different standards and channel conditions without requiring hardware modifications.

My PhD thesis presents efficient implementation methods (throughput, latency, energy consumption) for polar code decoding algorithms on specific hardware architectures (ASIP: Application Specific Instruction-set Processor) but also on general-purpose processors (GPP: General Purpose Processor). This work, in collaboration with Adrien Cassagne from LIP6, led to the creation of the open-source software AFF3CT, a complete software toolbox for the simulation and implementation of digital communication chains.

\subsection{SC-List and SCAN decoding algorithms}
\label{sec:sc_list_scan}

Polar codes, introduced by Arıkan in 2009, are the first error-correcting codes proven to achieve Shannon capacity for infinite code lengths~\cite{Arikan2009}. The Successive Cancellation (SC) decoding algorithm was initially proposed for polar codes, but its performance in terms of bit error rate (BER) and frame error rate (FER) is insufficient for short code lengths. To address this, the Successive Cancellation List (SCL) algorithm was proposed in the literature, significantly improving decoding performance by maintaining a list of candidate paths. An optimized version, called Fast SCL, was subsequently developed to reduce computational complexity while maintaining good decoding performance. These algorithms allow for trade-offs between complexity, latency, and decoding performance, making polar codes suitable for modern communication standards like 5G.

The polarization phenomenon and polar codes were developed assuming the use of the Successive Cancellation (SC) decoding algorithm. This is a hard-output algorithm, meaning the output data consists of bits. However, a second decoding algorithm, Belief Propagation (BP), was also proposed. This one is a soft-output algorithm. This means that the output data are estimates typically represented as LLRs (Log-Likelihood Ratios). As a reminder, in the LLR representation, the sign corresponds to the hard decision: a bit equal to 0 for a positive LLR, and a bit equal to 1 for a negative LLR. The absolute value indicates the reliability of this decision.

Although the SC algorithm is asymptotically optimal as N tends to infinity, its performance is limited for finite-sized codes. Numerous variants of these algorithms have therefore been proposed to improve their error correction performance. The Successive Cancellation List (SCL) algorithm is an evolution of the SC algorithm. Furthermore, the algorithm called Soft Cancellation (SCAN) can be seen as a combination of the SC and BP algorithms. Each of these algorithms is described in this section.

\begin{eqnarray}
  \begin{array}{l c l}
    f(L_a,L_b) &=& \text{sign}(L_a.L_b).\min(|L_a|,|L_b|)\\
    g(L_a,L_b,\hat{s}_a)&=&(1-2\hat{s}_a)L_a+L_b\\
    h(\hat{s}_a,\hat{s}_b)&=& (\hat{s}_{a} \oplus \hat{s}_{b}, \hat{s}_{b})\\
    \texttt{R0}(L_a) &=& 0 \\
    \texttt{R1}(L_a) &=&  \left\{\begin{array}{l c l} 0 \text{ si } L_a \geq 0 \\ 1 \text{ si } L_a < 0 \end{array}\right.
  \end{array}
  \label{eq:sc}
\end{eqnarray}
\paragraph{Successive Cancellation} decoding can be represented as a traversal of a binary tree. The tree representation, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:sc}, is an alternative representation that highlights certain algorithmic simplifications.
The data are organized in a binary tree with $log_2(N) + 1$ \textbf{levels}. In our pedagogical example, the tree has four levels.
At each level, a certain number of \textbf{nodes} are assigned.
The \textbf{root} of the tree, numbered $0$, consists of a single node.
The root contains $N$ LLRs and $N$ partial sums.
As the tree is traversed, at each level, the number of nodes doubles.
Conversely, the number of LLRs and partial sums in each node is halved.
The \textbf{leaves} are the nodes of the last level of the tree (level 3 in Figure~\ref{fig:sc}).
Processing a leaf corresponds to applying the \texttt{R0} and \texttt{R1} operations.
Each level $d$ contains $2^d$ nodes, each consisting of $2^{n-d}$ LLRs and $2^{n-d}$ partial sums, where $n=\log_2(N)$.
The partial sums are propagated by the function $h$. They are propagated up to a level that depends on the index of the leaf that has just been processed.
In the case of a non-systematic polar code, the decoded sequence $\hat{u}$ is composed of the partial sums contained in the leaves of the decoding tree.
During non-systematic decoding, the partial sums contained in the leaves correspond to the decoded sequence $\hat{u}$.

Figure~\ref{fig:seq_sc} is a more compact representation of the decoding tree.
Operations 12 and 13 in Figure~\ref{fig:seq_sc} are therefore redundant since they only serve to compute the partial sums of the root node.
In the case of a systematic code, it is the partial sums of the root node that correspond to the decoded codeword.
The traversal of the tree is a depth-first traversal.
The scheduling of the different functions is detailed in Figure~\ref{fig:seq_sc}, where each arrow representing a function is numbered according to the execution order.

\paragraph{Successive Cancellation List} (SCL) decoding algorithm is an extension of the SC algorithm~\cite{Tal2015}. The SC algorithm suffers from limited error-correction performance for short and moderate-length polar codes ($N < 8192$), whereas SCL decoding significantly improves these results.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{fig/scl}
\caption{Duplication de l'arbre de décodage dans l'algorithme SCL.}
\label{fig:scl}
\end{figure}

In the previously described SC decoder, hard decisions are applied whenever a leaf corresponding to an information bit is processed (operation \texttt{R1}). If an error is made at this stage, it cannot be corrected, and the decoding of the codeword fails. The key idea of list decoding is to postpone hard decisions. Instead of applying a threshold directly to the LLR, both possible decoding outcomes are explored. To do so, the decoding tree, together with all its LLRs and partial sums, is duplicated. This process is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:scl}.

The algorithm is shown at step (i), where the function $f$ is applied. At the next step (i+1), the decoding tree is duplicated to create two paths: one assuming $\hat{u}_2=0$, and the other assuming $\hat{u}_2=1$. Decoding then proceeds in parallel along both paths. At step (i+2), the function $g$ is applied. These two trees are referred to as decoding \textbf{paths}. At each leaf of rate 1, the same procedure is applied, doubling the number of paths to be decoded in parallel, as shown at step (i+3).

\paragraph{Concatenation with a CRC}

The authors of~\cite{Tal2015} proposed concatenating a Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC) to discriminate between the different candidates. The corresponding encoding is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:crc}. A CRC is an error-detecting code. It associates with any binary sequence, here $\bm{b}$ of length $K$, a sequence of length $K+c$. After decoding, the CRC verification allows the detection of potential errors. The CRC is designed so that the probability of an undetected error is extremely small.
Thus, at the end of the decoding algorithm, when the $\mathrm{L}$ candidate codewords at the output of the SCL algorithm are available, a CRC verification is performed. If one of them passes the CRC check, it is then highly probable that it is the correct candidate. In the rare case where several candidates pass the CRC check, the one with the smallest metric among them is selected. When this candidate selection mechanism based on CRC verification is applied, the algorithm is referred to as CRC-Aided SCL (CASCL).

\paragraph{Adaptive SCL Decoding Algorithms}
\label{subsubsec:adaptive}

The CASCL algorithm significantly improves decoding performance compared to the SC algorithm.
However, this improvement comes at the cost of increased computational complexity.
Nevertheless, it is possible to combine the two algorithms, SC and CASCL, in order to approach the low computational complexity of the former while preserving the decoding performance of the latter~\cite{Li2012}.
The principle is as follows: a first decoding attempt is performed with the SC algorithm. The CASCL algorithm is then triggered only if a decoding error is detected through CRC verification.

Two variants of the adaptive algorithm are possible.
In the case of the Partially Adaptive SCL (PASCL) algorithm presented in~\cite{Sarkis2016}, when SC decoding fails, the CASCL algorithm is executed only once with a list size of $\mathrm{L}_{max}$.
In the case of the Fully Adaptive SCL (FASCL) algorithm originally proposed in~\cite{Li2012}, the CASCL algorithm may be applied several times. The first CASCL decoding attempt is performed with $\mathrm{L}=2$. If, after decoding, the CRC is still not satisfied, the algorithm is relaunched as many times as necessary, iteratively doubling $\mathrm{L}$ until reaching $\mathrm{L}_{max}$.


\paragraph{Pruning of the polar decoding tree}
To reduce the computational complexity of the SC decoding algorithm, the decoding tree can be pruned as described in~\cite{Alamdar-Yazdi2011}. For instance, a subtree whose leaves are all rate-0 (frozen bits) does not need to be traversed, since all its partial sums are necessarily zero. Similarly, a subtree whose leaves are all rate-1 can be replaced by directly applying the thresholding function \texttt{R1} at its root, which yields the same result as a full traversal.

Beyond these two simple cases, other subtree patterns have also been identified, such as repetition (\texttt{REP}) and single parity check (\texttt{SPC}) nodes~\cite{Sarkis2014a}. For these structures, dedicated decoding techniques can be applied to derive the partial sums directly from the LLRs at the root, again avoiding traversal of the entire subtree.

The advantage of such pruning, often referred to as Fast-SC, is a drastic reduction of both computational and control complexity, which is especially relevant for software implementations where tree traversal is costly. Importantly, these techniques are not limited to SC decoding: they can be extended to list decoding as well, leading to the Fast-SCL algorithm~\cite{Sarkis2016}. The complexity reduction becomes particularly significant when the code rate is close to 0 or close to 1.

\subsection{A Fast and Flexible Software Implementation of Polar Code Decoders}
\label{sec:fast_flexible_polar_decoders}

Software implementation of channel codes has emerged as a compelling alternative to dedicated hardware. It enables rapid prototyping and validation of new algorithms, while offering high flexibility to support a wide variety of codes and decoding strategies. Unlike hardware, software decoders can be easily configured to trade off correction capability, latency, and throughput, making them well suited for adaptive schemes. They also facilitate reproducibility and portability across platforms. At the network level, software implementations align with the trend toward Cloud-RAN, where virtualized signal processing allows dynamic allocation of resources, improved inter-cell cooperation, reduced energy consumption, and lower communication latency.

The software implementation on General Purpose Processors (GPP) is a complete discipline, and it is not possible to present here the full set of methods, best practices, and techniques required to achieve good latency and throughput performance. Nevertheless, we highlight a few important techniques.

First, vectorization. Every modern GPP includes vector processing units, which are hardware modules within the processor capable of performing several identical computations in parallel on different (but contiguous) data. This is referred to as SIMD – Single Instruction Multiple Data. In polar decoding trees, these instructions are particularly useful to accelerate processing, as they allow several of the previously listed functions to be computed simultaneously when traversing from one node of the tree to another. Vectorization can be applied within a single decoding tree (intra-frame) or by processing several frames in parallel (inter-frame). The first case is more challenging since the number of processed data varies with the depth of the decoding tree. This number may even fall below the size of the vector registers at the lowest nodes, resulting in incomplete utilization of parallelism. In contrast, inter-frame parallelization does not suffer from this limitation, since the number of frames can be chosen to fully occupy the vector registers. However, its drawback lies in frame size: for large codewords, the cache capacity of the processors may be exceeded, leading to a collapse of performance.

A second technique, closely related to vectorization, is data quantization. It is possible to represent data in SC and SCL decoding trees with fewer than 32 bits, typically 16 or 8. This brings two main advantages. First, it increases the parallelism of vector instructions. Second, it reduces the memory footprint of the decoding trees, thereby decreasing cache usage and lowering the memory bandwidth requirements.

Finally, another notable technique is \emph{unrolling}. This consists in explicitly expanding the loops of the decoding algorithm in the source code, instead of relying on iterative loop execution. By doing so, the control overhead associated with loop counters and branching is eliminated, and the compiler can optimize the resulting straight-line code more aggressively. In the context of polar decoders, unrolling allows to reduce the latency of critical functions by removing conditional branches and improving instruction-level parallelism. However, the main drawbacks are, first, the increase in code size, which can become significant for large code lengths, and second, the lack of flexibility: for implementations supporting different code sizes or rates, dedicated source codes would need to be generated, which is not practical.


I proposed several major contributions for the software implementation of SCL decoders~\cite{leonardon:hal-01987848}.

\paragraph{Fine-grained Pruning Configuration}
I allowed fine control over tree pruning in our polar decoder implementations, enabling or disabling different node types (\texttt{R0}, \texttt{R1}, \texttt{REP}, \texttt{SPC}) independently. Figures~\ref{fig:nodes} and~\ref{fig:spc_impact} illustrate how enabling certain nodes affects coded throughput and decoding performance. High-rate codes benefit more from \texttt{R1} pruning, while low-rate codes gain more from \texttt{R0} and \texttt{REP} pruning; \texttt{SPC} nodes show a less clear trend. Allowing \texttt{SPC} nodes larger than 4 (\texttt{SPC4+}) increases throughput by 8–20% but may slightly degrade FER depending on the code and list depth. The pruning is dynamically activated, suggesting potential for a real-time adaptive pruning software decoder.

\paragraph{Fixed-Point Representation}
I implemented SCL decoders using 8- and 16-bit fixed-point representations for LLRs and path metrics to increase parallelism and improve throughput. Figure~\ref{fig:bfer_rep} shows that 16-bit fixed-point representation matches floating-point performance, while 8-bit representation can cause overflow in large repetition nodes (\texttt{REP}). Disabling large repetition nodes (\texttt{REP8-}) eliminates performance loss for 8-bit representation. Table~\ref{tab:quantif} demonstrates that fixed-point representations reduce latency and increase throughput, e.g., FASCL achieves 425.9 Mb/s with 8-bit LLRs. This allows efficient, high-throughput software implementations without significant loss in decoding performance.

\paragraph{Adaptive SCL Algorithm Variants}
Two adaptive SCL algorithms were implemented: Partially Adaptive (PASCL) and Fully Adaptive (FASCL), shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ascl_perfs}. PASCL is faster at low SNRs due to fewer intermediate decodings, while FASCL is superior at intermediate SNRs because it executes the algorithm with multiple intermediate list sizes. At high SNRs, both algorithms achieve similar throughput as SC decoding often suffices. Worst-case latency is higher for FASCL, as verified in Table~\ref{tab:quantif}. These implementations highlight the flexibility of our decoder, including dynamic pruning and support for different list-adaptive strategies.


\paragraph{Partial Sum Management}
We store partial sums in fixed-size integers matching LLR representation to exploit SIMD instructions. Memory footprint is reduced from $\mathrm{L}(2N-1)$ to $\mathrm{L}N$ for SCL by reusing memory when possible. We compare two assignment methods during duplication: copying ($SCL_{cpy}$) and pointer-based ($SCL_{ptr}$). $SCL_{cpy}$ is faster for small and medium codes ($N<8192$), while $SCL_{ptr}$ is more efficient for large codes, especially with 8-bit representation (Figure~\ref{fig:scl_mem}). This optimization reduces memory overhead and improves throughput depending on code size and data representation.

\paragraph{Partial Sorting Algorithm}
A new sorting method based on Schreier's tournament algorithm was proposed to select the smallest LLRs or path metrics during SCL execution~\cite{Schreier1932}. Compared to bubble sort, quick-sort, heap-sort, PSN networks, Schreier's method achieves the fastest execution in all tested scenarios, including \texttt{SPC} nodes. This simplifies the software design by using a single, efficient algorithm for all partial sorting operations. Although PSNs could theoretically improve throughput, we do not implement them due to the complexity of creating generic networks for any list size and pruning configuration.

\paragraph{Accelerated CRC Checking}
Profiling shows that CRC verification consumes significant execution time, as it is performed once per SC decoding and L times per SCL decoding. We accelerate CRC by packing 32 bits together, using precomputed CRC sequences, and exploiting pruning nodes for parallel extraction. For FASCL decoding of a polar code (2048,1723) with CRC-32 at $E_b/N_0=4.5$ dB, throughput increases from 262 Mb/s to 426 Mb/s (63\% gain). This approach reduces the computational complexity of CRC extraction and verification while integrating efficiently with SIMD operations.

\paragraph{Bit-packed Encoder}
\label{encoder}
The encoder implementation relies on bit-packing and proceeds in four stages. First, the input vector $U_N$, containing both frozen and information bits, is generated. This is done by statically identifying constituent nodes at compile time: rate-0 nodes are skipped, and large rate-1 nodes can be vectorized during memory copy. Second, $U_N$ is bit-packed using SIMD instructions, specifically \texttt{\_mm256\_cmpgt\_epi8} followed by \texttt{\_mm256\_movemask\_epi8}, which extracts the MSBs of each 8-bit element to form packed output. Third, systematic Polar encoding is performed using vectorized XOR and shift operations, applied intra- or inter-register depending on the node position in the encoding graph. Finally, unpacking is achieved using a 256-entry lookup table of 256-bit values.

\paragraph{Unrolled ASCL Decoder}
\label{unrolled}
The decoder implemented in this work follows the ASCL decoding principle. This approach aims to reduce the average latency of Polar decoders without compromising error-correction performance. Decoding begins with a fast SC decoder. If the decoded codeword passes the CRC check, it is accepted immediately. Otherwise, the decoder falls back to more powerful SCL decoders with increasing list sizes $L$. This adaptive strategy significantly lowers the average decoding time in high-SNR regimes, where SC decoding is often sufficient, while preserving the robustness of SCL decoding in more challenging conditions. The ASCL mechanism is well suited for short blocklength codes, where latency is critical.

To achieve low latency, we adopt a fully unrolled decoder generation strategy. For each Polar code configuration used in the contest, a dedicated and specialized C++ source file is generated. This eliminates control-flow overhead such as loops and recursive function calls, making the approach particularly effective for short blocklengths where latency is critical.
We provide an open-source decoder generator tool\footnote{Polar decoder generator: https://github.com/aff3ct/polar\_decoder\_gen}, which supports SC, SCL, and ASCL decoding modes. The tool produces optimized C++ code tailored to each frozen set, enabling reproducibility and integration into larger systems.

\subsection{AFF3CT: A Software Suite for Digital Communications}

AFF3CT is an open source software suite designed for the simulation and implementation of communication systems. At the time of its creation, it focused on Monte Carlo simulations of the whole communication chain: some data is generated, encoded, modulated, noised, decoded, and the performance is then estimated by measuring the Bit Error Rate (BER) and the Frame Error Rate (FER). This process led to 3 problems that are addressed by the tool:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Simulation time:} In order to accurately estimate the FER/BER, at least 100 erroneous frames must be simulated. For example, measuring a FER of $10^{-7}$ requires simulating the transmission of approximately $100 \times 10^{7} = 10^{9}$ frames. Assuming a frame length of 1000 bits, the simulator must emulate the transmission of $10^{12}$ bits. Considering that decoding algorithms may be computationally demanding, several weeks or even months of simulation may be required to obtain accurate FER/BER estimates for a given coding scheme.

  \item \textbf{Algorithmic heterogeneity:} A wide variety of channel codes have been developed over time. For each type of code, several decoding algorithms may be available. While describing a single coding scheme is relatively straightforward, providing a unified software framework that supports multiple coding schemes and their associated algorithms is much more challenging. This difficulty stems from the heterogeneity of the data structures required to represent codes and decoders: turbo codes rely on trellises, LDPC codes are naturally expressed on factor graphs, and polar codes are efficiently decoded using binary trees.

  \item \textbf{Reproducibility:} Reproducing published results is often a tedious task. This is partly due to the large number of empirical parameters required to specify a communication system, not all of which are systematically reported in publications. Moreover, simulator source codes are rarely made publicly available. As a consequence, significant time is frequently spent ``reinventing the wheel'' merely to enable comparison with state-of-the-art results.
\end{enumerate}



The long simulation times make it desirable to have \textbf{high throughput
implementations}. The algorithmic heterogeneity requires \textbf{flexible,
modular software}. The reproducibility issue pushes towards a \textbf{portable}
and \textbf{open-source software}. These are the purposes of \AFFECT.

\subsubsection{Library}

As a FEC library, \AFFECT can be used programmatically, for instance in
\emph{Software Defined Radio} (SDR) contexts or in simulations. \AFFECT blocks
can be used in external projects without restriction. Compute intensive blocks
are optimized and vectorized to run fast on a single core. The library is
thread-safe; however, it is not multi-threaded by itself, in contrast to the
simulator. Instead, it is the responsibility of the user to manage
multi-threading.

\subsubsection{Software Architecture}
\label{sec:soft_archi}

\AFFECT is developed in \Cxx in an object-oriented programming style. It
provides the fundamental blocks involved in building communication chains
(sources, modems, codecs, channels, ...). Those blocks are organized as
\textit{modules} and \textit{tasks}.

\textbf{Module:} A set of related tasks sharing some characteristics. For
instance, the \textit{modem} module contains the \textit{modulate} and
\textit{demodulate} tasks.

\textbf{Task:} An elementary processing performed on some data. For instance,
\textit{decode} or \textit{modulate} are tasks. The tasks are characterized by
their \textit{sockets}. A socket of a task defines an entry point through which
the task will consume and/or produce data. There are three kinds of sockets:
\textit{input}, \textit{output} and \textit{input/output}, following a
philosophy close to \emph{ports} in component-based development approaches.

\begin{figure}[htp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[scale=0.400]{fig/tasks_modules.pdf}
  \caption{Modules and tasks.}
  \label{fig:modules_tasks}
\end{figure}

As a rule, a task is always a \emph{verb} and a module is always a \emph{noun}.
Modules are implemented as C++ classes, and tasks as class methods. \AFFECT
defines several abstract modules, for sources, codecs, modems, channels, etc. It
readily provides many implementations of those abstract classes; it is also
straightforward to add new ones. Figure~\ref{fig:modules_tasks} presents common
modules and tasks typically found in a basic communication chain. It shows that
the number of tasks per module can vary depending on the module type.

During my ATER position at the University of Bordeaux, our research group was
contacted by Airbus Defense and Space to develop a software-defined radio (SDR)
implementation of the DVB-S2 standard. The goal was to create a real-time
demonstrator capable of streaming video over Universal Software Radio Peripheral
(USRP) platforms. This demonstrator serves to showcase the \AFFECT toolbox and
its capabilities in practical SDR applications.

The collaboration led to the development of a complete end-to-end DVB-S2
transceiver, including both the transmission and reception chains. In addition
to implementing the standard coding and modulation schemes, a key contribution
of this work was the integration of robust synchronization functionalities,
which were not available in \AFFECT at the time. This enabled reliable frame,
timing, and frequency recovery under real-world transmission conditions. The
developed system is still used today to demonstrate \AFFECT toolsuite features
and to illustrate high-performance SDR processing on general-purpose multicore
CPUs.

Software implementations of digital communication standards such as DVB-S2 are
particularly relevant in satellite ground stations. They offer flexibility,
hardware mutualization, and maintainability advantages over dedicated hardware
solutions, especially when the production volume is low or when the system must
adapt to evolving standards. However, achieving real-time performance in
software is challenging, as the system must process tens of megabits per second
with minimal latency while compensating for imperfections in the transmission
chain, such as frequency offsets and timing drifts.

In this context, the proposed SDR implementation combines the flexibility of
software with the performance required for real-time operations. It relies on
the parallelism of multicore CPUs and Single Instruction Multiple Data (SIMD)
vectorization to accelerate processing tasks. The demonstrator supports multiple
DVB-S2 MODulation and CODing schemes (MODCODs) and provides precise
synchronization through dedicated software modules.

The remainder of this section presents the overall DVB-S2 transceiver
architecture, detailing the transmission and reception chains as illustrated in
Figs.~\ref{fig:sdr_dvbs2_transmitter},~\ref{fig:sdr_dvbs2_receiver_learning}
and~\ref{fig:sdr_dvbs2_receiver_transmission}. This description emphasizes both
the processing steps inherited from \AFFECT and the newly implemented modules
for synchronization, filtering, and real-time sample handling.

\subsubsection{DVB-S2 Transceiver Overview}


The developed DVB-S2 transceiver is composed of two main parts: the transmitter
and the receiver, both implemented in software and interfaced with USRP
hardware for analog signal transmission and reception. The design follows a
modular approach, where each processing task is clearly separated and can be
optimized or parallelized independently.

\paragraph{Transmitter Architecture}

The transmitter chain, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:sdr_dvbs2_transmitter},
reads the source data and applies the DVB-S2 processing steps, including
encoding, modulation, and optional interleaving. Additional tasks such as
scrambling, pilot insertion, and shaping filtering ensure that the transmitted
signal meets the standard requirements and facilitates synchronization at the
receiver. Some tasks rely directly on existing \AFFECT modules, while others
have been implemented specifically for the SDR context, allowing real-time
transmission through the USRP.

\paragraph{Receiver Architecture}

The receiver chain, depicted in
Figs.~\ref{fig:sdr_dvbs2_receiver_learning}~and~\ref{fig:sdr_dvbs2_receiver_transmission},
follows a phased processing approach. Initially, the receiver waits for an
incoming signal and performs acquisition in several stages to estimate timing,
frequency, and frame alignment. Once synchronization is achieved, the
transmission phase begins, in which the incoming frames are processed in real
time through the full DVB-S2 decoding chain.

Dedicated synchronization tasks ensure resilience to frequency offsets, timing
drifts, and phase variations in the received signal. These tasks, along with
filters and frame extractors, were newly implemented for this SDR demonstrator
and are integrated with the \AFFECT interfaces, providing a modular and
instrumented software architecture.

\paragraph{Key Features}

The SDR implementation highlights several important aspects:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Real-time processing:} The receiver pipeline is carefully
        parallelized and optimized to handle the high data rates imposed by the
        USRP modules.
  \item \textbf{Flexible and modular design:} The transceiver architecture
        separates tasks in a way that facilitates adaptation to different
        MODCODs and transmission scenarios.
  \item \textbf{Synchronization:} Custom software modules compensate for
        timing and frequency drifts, enabling robust reception under realistic
        conditions.
  \item \textbf{Integration with \AFFECT:} The design leverages the toolbox for
        core signal processing and decoding tasks, while extending it with SDR
        specific functionality.
\end{itemize}

This high-level architecture demonstrates that a software-only DVB-S2
transceiver can meet real-time constraints using general-purpose CPUs,
multicore parallelism, and SIMD acceleration, while remaining fully modular and
extensible.
\subsubsection{Real-Time Implementation Features}
\label{sec:real_time_summary}

Achieving real-time performance for a software DVB-S2 transceiver requires
careful management of data flow, processing parallelism, and hardware
capabilities. In our SDR demonstrator, the following key strategies were applied:

\paragraph{Reliable Sample Collection and Delivery}

The USRP modules impose strict constraints on sample throughput and jitter.
To avoid underflows and overflows, the radio threads are pinned to dedicated
CPU cores and cores are isolated using the Linux \texttt{isolcpus} option.
Large host-side buffers and a copy-less, lock-free producer/consumer pipeline
ensure continuous delivery and collection of samples at high rates.

\paragraph{Vectorized Processing with SIMD}

Tasks such as synchronization and filtering are heavily vectorized using SIMD
instructions. Complex numbers are represented in an array-of-structures format,
and the MIPP library is employed to maintain portability across CPU
architectures (x86 AVX-512 and ARM NEON). Fused Multiply-Add (FMA) instructions
are used in filters to accelerate linear algebra computations.


\paragraph{High-Throughput Pipeline and Parallelization}

The receiver is decomposed into sequential and parallel pipeline stages,
allowing multi-threaded execution of computationally intensive tasks. The
pipeline design ensures that slower sequential stages, such as the
\emph{Synchronizer Timing} and BCH decoder, do not block the overall throughput
more than necessary. Stage duplication is applied for highly parallelizable
tasks to maximize CPU utilization, with careful memory placement for NUMA
systems.

\section{ASIP Architectures for Polar Codes}
Following the work on software implementations of polar code decoding on general-purpose processors (GPP), two contributions explored performance optimization through specialized programmable processors (ASIP - Application Specific Instruction-set Processor). This paradigm shift is in line with the Cloud-RAN logic, where the virtualization of physical functions allows great flexibility in resource allocation. Nevertheless, to achieve high throughput and energy efficiency while maintaining a certain level of programmability, the use of ASIPs proves particularly relevant. Unlike GPPs, which are often oversized and energy-intensive, ASIPs allow for fine-tuning of hardware resources to the target algorithm, while integrating vector processing units or specific instructions to improve efficiency.

\subsection{Tensilica} The first work relied on the Cadence Tensilica ecosystem to design a processor based on the Xtensa LX7 core, adapted for SC and SCAN (Soft CANcellation) polar code decoding~\cite{leonardon:hal-03291077}. This processor was configured to include a VLIW pipeline, SIMD instructions on 512-bit registers, and a reduced set of general-purpose registers optimized for LLR and partial sum access. The approach combined architectural optimizations (custom instructions, adapted memory management, quantized arithmetic) and software optimizations (pseudo-unrolling of decoding, use of specialized functions like R0/R1/REP/SPC). The results showed that throughput was comparable to that obtained on an ARM Cortex-A57, with a 10-fold reduction in energy consumption, thus demonstrating the relevance of the approach for embedded applications or those deployed on Cloud-RAN servers requiring strong energy efficiency constraints.

\subsection{Transport Triggered Architecture}The second article proposes a significant evolution of ASIP-based polar decoding through the use of a \textit{Transport Triggered Architecture} (TTA), developed in collaboration with the University of Tampere, which also motivated the project visit with Pekka Jääskeläinen via Maupertuis funding~\cite{leonardon:hal-01978830}.
The Transport Triggered Architecture (TTA) is a modular processor architecture template \cite{corporaal_microprocessor_1997}, inspired by the MOVE architecture \cite{1051344}. To facilitate the design and implementation of TTA-based systems, a dedicated hardware/software development toolchain, known as the TTA Co-Design Environment (TCE), has been developed \cite{Esko:2010:CED:1933303.1933795}.

A TTA consists of elementary components, called function units (FUs), interconnected through a configurable number of buses. A Base TTA comprises a control unit (GCU) handling fetch and decode operations, an arithmetic logic unit (ALU), a load/store unit (LSU), and a register file (RF). The modularity of the architecture allows designers to add custom FUs and to expand the interconnection network with additional buses.
Unlike conventional processor architectures, a TTA does not rely on a fixed instruction set. Instead, computation is triggered by data movements: writing data to a particular input port, known as the \textit{trigger port}, initiates the corresponding operation within the FU. The executed “instruction” is thus a side effect of the data transfer. The architecture inherently supports parallelism, allowing multiple data transfers to occur simultaneously. Consequently, programming a TTA entails specifying, for each clock cycle, the source and destination of all concurrent data movements, effectively exposing the processor’s datapath to the programmer.

While this exposed datapath provides flexibility, manual programming at the assembly level would be extremely tedious. TCE addresses this challenge by providing a retargetable compiler that, given an architecture description, translates high-level code (C, C++, OpenCL) into executable TTA programs \cite{Esko:2010:CED:1933303.1933795}. Program efficiency is enhanced by leveraging the modular LLVM infrastructure \cite{lattner_llvm:_2004}, which handles frontend and middle-end optimizations, while TTA-specific low-level optimizations are applied through a custom code generation library integrated into TCE.
A fully customized TTA processor, including its bespoke function units, can be automatically generated as either generic or optimized RTL (VHDL or Verilog). The resulting processor can then be directly synthesized and implemented on ASIC or FPGA platforms. Thanks to their modularity and flexibility, TTA-based designs have demonstrated elegant and efficient solutions across a wide range of applications.

\subsubsection{Transport Triggered Polar Decoders} The proposed architecture, illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:prode}, builds upon a Base TTA and extends it with vector custom function units (FUs) to enable highly parallel processing. A vector size of $P=64$ was selected, allowing the FUs to process up to 64 data elements (LLRs or PSs) simultaneously. LLRs are represented as $Q=8$-bit values, ensuring negligible decoding performance loss. Core components of the architecture include parallel Load/Store Units (LSUs) for efficient memory access, Polar Processing Units (PPUs) that implement the specific $f$ and $g$ polar functions, and an unrolled SC decoder designed to accelerate the most sequential parts of polar decoding. A Vector Register File is included to buffer vector data and facilitate smooth data transfers between FUs.

The LSUs are tailored for high throughput, supporting the maximum polar code length of $N_\text{max}=1024$ as defined in the 5G standard \cite{ETSI2018}. PS memory is organized as 32x64 bits to allow parallel access to $P=64$ data, while LLR memory is mapped into a 64x512-bit RAM. The LSUs are optimized to reduce load/store latency from three to one clock cycle by removing internal registers, and they also support unaligned memory accesses without affecting the critical path, which remains in the 32-bit scalar ALU.

The PPUs, comprising the PS PU and LLR PU, act as SIMD units specialized for polar decoding, allowing multiple $f$ and $g$ operations to be performed in parallel. To further accelerate computation, unrolled subtree decoders are integrated as FUs for the lower stages of the decoding tree. These unrolled SC decoders can process 8 LLRs in 6 clock cycles, using a multi-cycle timing strategy instead of additional registers. This approach removes unnecessary hardware, keeps the critical path in the general-purpose ALU, and maintains full flexibility for varying frozen bit patterns. Overall, the architecture leverages vectorized FUs, efficient memory access, and unrolled subtree processing to achieve high-performance polar decoding while preserving the programmability and modularity of the Base TTA.

\subsubsection{Results} A fine-grain comparison of polar decoders is challenging due to differences in technology, algorithms, pruning strategies, quantization, and frozen bit locations. Nevertheless, the Transport Triggered Polar Decoder (TT-SC), customized for SC decoding, demonstrates that TTA provides a natural compromise between programmable and dedicated architectures. Compared to previous work, the proposed TT-SC processor achieves significantly higher throughput and energy efficiency.

The TT-SC decoder was implemented using a 28 nm FD-SOI library, with memories realized in SRAM blocks. For a (1024, 512) polar code, it achieves a throughput of 352 Mb/s, which is 37\% higher than an Intel i7 CPU implementation, while consuming only 0.14 nJ/bit, two orders of magnitude lower than the general-purpose solution. Although more clock cycles are required than in dedicated architectures due to pipeline registration and more complete tree pruning, the TT-SC maintains comparable logic utilization and competitive hardware complexity while preserving programmability. Overall, the TT-SC processor illustrates that a TTA-based approach can deliver high-performance polar decoding while retaining flexibility.

\section{Low-Cost Federated Learning}
\label{sec:federated_learning}
\subsection{Federated Learning Overview}


Data privacy has become a critical concern in domains like high-frequency trading, marketing, and machine learning. Centralized data collection can expose sensitive information to interception or unauthorized access. Federated learning (FL)~\cite{konevcny2015federated} addresses these concerns by enabling machine learning models to be trained on decentralized data stored across multiple participant devices, called \textbf{clients}, without sharing raw data. The main objective is to mitigate privacy violations while leveraging distributed data.

In a typical FL setup, a central server orchestrates training by selecting clients, sending them the current model, and aggregating their local updates. This process, repeated in multiple \textbf{communication rounds}, involves client selection, local training, model update uploads, and aggregation. The aggregation fuses knowledge from all participating clients into a global model.


Federated learning can be characterized along several dimensions~\cite{kairouz2021advances}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Orchestration:} Centralized (server coordinates updates) or decentralized (peer-to-peer).
    \item \textbf{Synchronization:} Synchronous (waits for all clients) or asynchronous (updates as received).
    \item \textbf{Client type:} Cross-device (many mobile/edge clients, limited resources) or cross-silo (few institutions, reliable and resourceful).
    \item \textbf{Client statefulness:} Stateful (retains inter-round information) or stateless (no retained info).
    \item \textbf{Data distribution:} IID (similar across clients) or non-IID (highly heterogeneous). Non-IID distributions create challenges like client drift.
    \item \textbf{Data partition:} Horizontal (same features, different samples) or vertical (different features, same samples).
\end{itemize}

This work focuses on deep learning image classification using CNNs, with centralized orchestration, synchronous updates, stateless cross-device clients, IID and non-IID data, and horizontal partitions.

\subsubsection{Training Loop and Federated Averaging}

A federated learning round consists of multiple coordinated steps involving the server and a set of participating clients. The overall loop is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:chap2:loop}. The key steps are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Client Sampling:}
    At the start of each round $t$, the server selects a subset $M \subset U$ of clients from the total pool $U$ to participate. This sampling is critical for scalability, especially in cross-device settings with thousands or millions of potential clients, and also reduces the likelihood of stragglers slowing down the round.

    \item \textbf{Model Broadcast:}
    The server sends the current global model $\mathcal{W}^{(t)}$ to each selected client. This model can include weights, biases, or other relevant parameters depending on the architecture.

    \item \textbf{Local Training:}
    Each client $m \in M$ trains the received model locally on its private dataset $d_m$. The objective is to minimize the client-specific loss function:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_m(\mathcal{W}_m) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim d_m}[\mathcal{L}(x,y,\mathcal{W}_m)].
    \end{equation}
    Clients perform training over multiple local epochs with a specified batch size. This local training allows each client to adapt the global model to its specific data distribution while keeping raw data private.

    \item \textbf{Update Upload:}
    After local training, clients compute their model updates, which can be the updated weights $\Delta \mathcal{W}_m$, gradients, or other proxy information. These updates are sent back to the server, not the raw data. This ensures that sensitive information remains local.

    \item \textbf{Aggregation:}
    The server aggregates all client updates to produce the next global model. The most widely used method is \textbf{Federated Averaging (FedAvg)}~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}, which computes a weighted average based on dataset sizes:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{W}^{(t+1)} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|d_m|}{|D|} \Delta \mathcal{W}_m,
    \end{equation}
    where $|D| = \sum_{m=1}^{M} |d_m|$. This aggregation fuses knowledge from heterogeneous client distributions into a coherent global model.

    \item \textbf{Iteration:}
    The updated global model is then used in the next round. This process repeats for $R$ rounds until the global model converges to a desired performance level.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{equation}
    \underset{\mathcal{W}}{\mathrm{min}}~V(\mathcal{W}) = \sum\limits_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|d_m|}{|D|} \mathcal{L}_{m}(\mathcal{W}_m)
\end{equation}

Experiments with 20 clients training ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10 over 100 rounds demonstrate that IID distributions achieve $\sim 92\%$ accuracy, whereas non-IID achieve $\sim 81\%$.

\subsubsection{Challenges in Cross-Device Federated Learning}

Cross-device FL introduces challenges in computation and communication. Devices often have limited processing power, memory, and energy, making deep learning training resource-intensive. Frequent model exchanges increase network load and energy consumption. Reducing communication rounds improves convergence and mitigates straggling clients but may increase local computation.

A study~\cite{qiu2023first} evaluated energy costs for CIFAR-10 and ImageNet using ResNet-18:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CIFAR-10:} Centralized training consumed 2.7 Wh; federated training across 500 clients totaled 30.4 Wh (3.04 Wh per client). Communication accounted for 96\% of energy.
    \item \textbf{ImageNet:} Centralized 971 Wh; federated across 100 clients totaled 2697.5 Wh (269.5 Wh per client). Communication was only 0.7\% due to larger dataset size.
\end{itemize}

This highlights that dataset size and task complexity significantly affect communication costs in federated learning. Model compression and convergence optimization are potential strategies to reduce energy and communication overhead.

\subsection{FLOCORA: Federated Learning Compression with LORA}
\paragraph{Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)}

A recent approach to reduce training and compression costs relies on \textit{Low-Rank Adaptation} (LoRA). The main idea is to avoid updating all parameters of a neural network during training, and instead restrict updates to \textit{low-rank matrices} that approximate the necessary parameter changes.

Consider a weight matrix of a layer:
\[
W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_2}.
\]
In standard training, this matrix is updated directly via gradient descent. In LoRA, however, the matrix $W$ is \textit{frozen}, and the update is represented as a low-rank decomposition:
\[
W^{*} = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA,
\]
where
\[
A \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times r}, \quad B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_2},
\]
are trainable matrices with rank at most $r$, and $\alpha$ is a scaling factor. Since $r \ll \min(d_1, d_2)$, the number of additional parameters to train is drastically reduced.

This approach was originally motivated by \textit{transfer learning} on very large models, in particular \textit{large language models} (LLMs) with hundreds of billions of parameters. Rather than fine-tuning the entire model, LoRA allows adapting a pretrained backbone to new tasks with a fraction of the memory and compute cost. An additional advantage is that the low-rank updates can be merged back into the original weights without adding inference latency.

In summary, LoRA addresses several key challenges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training efficiency}: only the low-rank matrices are optimized, reducing memory and computation requirements.
    \item \textbf{Parameter efficiency}: the pretrained backbone can be reused, while task-specific information is stored in lightweight adapters.
    \item \textbf{Communication efficiency}: since the backbone remains frozen, only low-rank updates need to be transmitted, which is especially relevant in distributed or federated settings.
\end{itemize}

Although originally developed for \textit{natural language processing (NLP)} and \textit{foundation models}, LoRA has rapidly become a standard tool in efficient deep learning, and its extension to other domains (computer vision, multimodal learning, federated learning) is an active area of research.

\paragraph{Our Method: FLoCoRA}

In this work, we propose \textbf{FLoCoRA} (\textbf{F}ederated \textbf{L}earning \textbf{Co}mpression with \textbf{Lo}w-\textbf{R}ank \textbf{A}daptation), a method that integrates LoRA into the federated learning (FL) framework to reduce both communication and memory requirements.

The key idea of FLoCoRA is to \textit{never update the original neural network weights} shared across clients at the start of training. Instead, only the LoRA adapter parameters, $A$ and $B$, are trained, exchanged, and aggregated. Because the original weights remain frozen, communicating only the low-rank adapters is sufficient to represent each client's updates. This design allows FLoCoRA to be seamlessly integrated with any FL optimization algorithm.

Figure~\ref{fedavglora} illustrates the FLoCoRA training loop. All clients start with identical weights $W_{\text{initial}}$, which remain fixed throughout training. During a communication round, the server first sends the global LoRA adapter parameters $\bar{\Delta}_t L$ to the selected subset of clients. Each client then trains its LoRA adapters locally and computes the updates $\Delta^k_{t+1} L$, which are uploaded back to the server. The server aggregates the updates using a weighted average, as in FedAvg, to obtain the new global adapters $\bar{\Delta}_{t+1} L$ for the next round.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centerline{
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{fig/fedavglora.pdf}}
    \caption{FLoCoRA training loop.}
    \label{fedavglora}
\end{figure}

By training only the low-rank adapters, FLoCoRA reduces memory usage since fewer gradients need to be stored during backpropagation. Communication costs are lowered because only the adapter parameters are transmitted between clients and the server, and computational cost per round is reduced because the majority of the model weights remain frozen. FLoCoRA can also be combined with quantization techniques to further decrease communication overhead without significant loss in accuracy.

\section{Compression by Pruning or Quantization and Embedded Deployment}
\label{sec:compression}



Another set of contributions focused on neural network pruning and was carried out as part of Hugo Tessier's thesis, in collaboration with Matthieu Arzel and Vincent Gripon from IMT Atlantique, thanks to CIFRE funding from the company Stellantis, with Thomas Hannagan and David Bertrand as supervisors.

\subsection{Pruning Neural Networks and Hardware Acceleration}

\paragraph{Principle}

The basic idea of pruning is to reduce the cost of a neural network by removing part of its structure. Hugo has introduced in a popular blog post a comprehensive overview of this technique\footnote{\url{https://towardsdatascience.com/neural-network-pruning-101-af816aaea61/}}. The intuitive principle of pruning naturally raises three questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Which elements of the network can be removed, and what impact does this removal have on its cost?
    \item How can one identify the elements whose removal affects performance the least?
    \item What is the most effective way to remove these elements?
\end{itemize}
These questions respectively define three complementary dimensions of pruning: the pruning structure, the pruning criterion, and the pruning method.

\paragraph{Structures}

The pruning structure determines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item which type of cost pruning will reduce;
    \item how easily pruning can be exploited in practice;
    \item the granularity of pruning, and thus the ability to prune significantly without degrading performance.
\end{itemize}

The most basic form of pruning is non-structured pruning, where individual weights are removed according to a given rule. This approach provides an excellent trade-off between performance and number of parameters, and is relatively simple to implement. However, it also presents two major drawbacks: it generates sparse tensors that are difficult to leverage efficiently on hardware, and it does not reduce tensor sizes, so intermediate representations remain unaffected.

To address these limitations, many approaches prune entire convolutional filters, a strategy usually referred to as structured pruning. Although this coarser granularity leads to a less favorable performance-to-parameters trade-off, it has two significant advantages: it actually reduces the size of weight tensors (making it compatible with most hardware and frameworks), and it decreases the size of intermediate representations, thereby lowering runtime memory requirements.

\paragraph{Criteria}

Once the pruning structure is chosen, the pruning criterion determines which elements are most relevant to remove. The guiding principle is to discard those elements that contribute the least to the accuracy of the model. Since direct evaluation of each parameter’s importance is infeasible in large networks, surrogate metrics are employed. The two most common types are magnitude-based criteria (absolute value of weights) and gradient-based criteria.

When criteria are applied to groups of weights, two main tactics are used: either computing the norm of the metric across the group, or relying on proxies such as batch-normalization scaling factors, which are assumed to reflect the relative importance of upstream filters.

Another important dimension concerns how pruning is distributed across layers. Strategies include uniform pruning across all layers, manually defined layer-wise pruning rates, or a global target applied to the network as a whole. Moreover, since layers differ in size, the definition of the pruning rate may vary: some works consider the fraction of pruned filters, while others focus on the resulting number of remaining parameters.

\paragraph{Methods}

The pruning method specifies how elements selected by the criterion are actually removed. Two intertwined aspects are considered: (i) the removal strategy and (ii) the pruning schedule.

The removal strategy can be definitive (setting weights permanently to zero) or dynamic (allowing regrowth, reparameterization via masks, or continuous penalization).
The schedule defines when pruning occurs relative to training: after training with alternating fine-tuning, progressively during training, or through re-training strategies with or without weight reinitialization.

\paragraph{Contributions}

This manuscript introduces several contributions in the field of pruning. Since they are sometimes combined, we briefly summarize them here before detailing them later.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Selective Weight Decay (SWD)}: a method that prunes weights continuously during training, using an increasing penalty until their values reach zero. SWD is compatible with any pruning structure and criterion, and its adaptive nature allows efficient pruning even at very high compression rates.
    \item \textbf{Dimensional Clear-Out (DCO)}: a framework addressing the structural inconsistencies induced by structured pruning. DCO identifies dependencies between layers and operations, removes disconnected weights, and adapts broken operations (e.g., mismatched tensor dimensions in residual connections), ensuring that inference remains possible under arbitrary structured pruning distributions.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation of structured pruning on GPU}: a study showing how cost metrics influence the perceived efficiency of pruning and highlighting the risks of imbalance in pruning criteria when deployed on hardware.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Selective Weight Decay (SWD)}


SWD aims at pruning as smoothly as possible by applying an additional $\mathcal{L}_2$ penalty on the subset $\mathbf{W}^*$ of weights to prune from $\mathbf{W}$.
The standard optimization problem with weight decay can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
    \min_{\mathbf{W}} \; \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}), \mathbf{y})
    \quad \text{with} \quad
    \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}), \mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}), \mathbf{y}) + \mu \left\| \mathbf{W} \right\|_2,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ is the supervised training loss and $\mu$ the weight decay factor.

We extend this formulation by introducing Selective Weight Decay in Equation~\ref{eq:swd_loss}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:swd_loss}
    \min_{\mathbf{W}} \; \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}), \mathbf{y})
    \quad \text{with} \quad
    \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}), \mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}), \mathbf{y}) + \mu \left\| \mathbf{W} \right\|_2 + a \mu \left\| \mathbf{W}^* \right\|_2.
\end{equation}

This additional $\mathcal{L}_2$ penalty, weighted by the multiplier $a$, can be seen as a relaxation of an $\mathcal{L}_0$ constraint, progressively forcing the targeted weights toward zero. This principle is directly inspired by Lagrangian smoothing~\cite{murray2010algorithm}. Figure~\ref{fig:swd_illustration} illustrates the landscape of the combined penalty of weight decay and SWD.

The subset $\mathbf{W}^*$ represents the weights to prune. Its definition depends on the chosen pruning structure and criterion, which are orthogonal to SWD itself, as SWD is only a removal method. A key property is that $\mathbf{W}^*$ can be updated dynamically during training. In practice, we chose to update $\mathbf{W}^*$ at each training step, although less frequent updates are theoretically possible.

\begin{figure}[t]
      \centering
      \subfile{fig/swd_illustration}
      \caption{In the case of non-structured magnitude pruning, SWD behaves as such: all weights $w$ of $\mathbf{W}$ are applied a penalty $P$, that combines weight decay and SWD. Since SWD is only applied to weights below a given threshold $t$, it is like $P$ applies a stronger penalty on weights below $t$ and a smaller one above $t$. Another way to present this is to consider SWD as a trap that progressively subjects weights below a certain threshold to an increasingly strong penalty, that drives them to zero, thus pruning them.}
      \label{fig:swd_illustration}
  \end{figure}
The multiplier $a$ controls the strength of the selective penalty. Its value must grow during training so that the targeted weights are eventually forced toward zero, while avoiding divergence of the optimization dynamics. We interpolate $a$ between a starting value $a_{min}$ and a final value $a_{max}$ using an exponential schedule:
\begin{equation}
	a(s) = a_{min} \left( \frac{a_{max}}{a_{min}} \right)^{\frac{s}{s_{final}}},
	\label{eq:a_definition}
\end{equation}
with $s$ the current training step and $s_{final}$ the total number of pruning steps. Ablation studies on the influence of $a$ have been conducted in our previous work~\cite{tessier:hal-03675138}, and we therefore do not reproduce them in detail here.

In this manuscript I focus on the most significant results which are on ImageNet, but further results on CIFAR10, City Scapes on various network architectures are available in Hugo's thesis~\cite{tessier:tel-04064137}. CIFAR-10 allows finer-grained exploration: it shows extensive trade-off curves on two variants of ResNet-20, one over-parameterized and easy to prune (initial width of 64 channels) and one much more parsimonious and sensitive to pruning (initial width of 16 channels). Finally, Cora serves to evaluate SWD on a graph-based task, namely node classification. Indeed, a great advantage of SWD is that it applies easily on any network architecture, pruning method, pruning structure.

ImageNet provides large-scale evaluation, albeit at high computational cost. Table~\ref{tab:imagenet_table} reports top-1 accuracy for both non-structured and structured pruning. SWD consistently achieves the best performance, particularly under aggressive pruning. For example, at 80\% non-structured sparsity, SWD reaches 72.4\% top-1 accuracy, compared to 68.7\% for the baseline and 70.1\% for LRR; at 90\% sparsity, SWD maintains 68.2\%, while the baseline drops to 60.5\% and LRR to 63.0\%. For structured pruning of filters, removing 50\% of filters results in 75.1\% accuracy for SWD, versus 73.2\% for the baseline and 74.0\% for LRR.

Two main tendencies are observed. First, SWD preserves substantial accuracy under high pruning rates where other methods fail. Second, the LRR variant~\cite{renda2020comparing} occasionally degrades the baseline’s performance while improving SWD, a behavior not seen in other experiments. The cause of this discrepancy remains unclear, suggesting that SWD and the baseline produce pruned architectures with differing retraining dynamics, although this does not fully explain why pruned and fine-tuned networks outperform warm-restarted counterparts.



\paragraph{Dimensional Clear-Out (DCO)}

From an implementation perspective, it is clear that structured pruning techniques can be effectively applied to reduce the dimensionality of neural networks and therefore increase their throughput, decrease their latency, improve their power efficiency. Removing filters reduces the size of layers, which any framework can leverage. Even though it is perfectly true, there are some problems that we did not mention and that have a significant impact on how to perform structured pruning.



Filter pruning is complicated by interdependencies between layers. In a network, each filter produces a feature map, and the filters of subsequent layers must match these input dimensions. Pruning a filter therefore requires removing the corresponding kernels in downstream layers. This explains why filter pruning is often referred to as ``channel pruning''. Residual connections further complicate pruning, since tensors to be summed must have matching dimensions. In ResNet-like architectures, these dimensions depend on complex combinations of upstream layers, making it difficult to predict the exact size of intermediate representations.

\begin{figure}[ht]
     \centering
     \subfile{fig/pruning_problems}
     \caption{Example of problems introduced by pruning in the presence of residual connections.}
     \label{fig:pruning_problems}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pruning_problems} illustrates typical issues: pruned filters alter output dimensions, requiring adjustments in subsequent layers. Residual additions may need to be replaced by concatenations to maintain correct tensor shapes. The input dimensions of layers, and thus the number of parameters, depend on the global pruning pattern. Ignoring these dependencies may cause runtime errors or force pruning to be simulated by zeroing weights rather than actually removing them.

A related issue is \emph{orphan biases}. When a filter is removed but its bias remains, it produces a constant output. This can prevent downstream kernels from being removed, reducing the effective compression and potentially altering performance if fine-tuning is not applied. Biases are often stored in batch-normalization layers, so pruning only the convolution layer may leave the bias intact. Furthermore, secondary removals (following layers’ kernels) can be overlooked, leading to incomplete pruning.

Together, layer interdependencies and orphan biases make proper filter pruning subtle and challenging. In complex networks such as HRNet, these issues motivate the need for automatic, generic methods to identify dependencies and adjust operators, ensuring correct and effective pruning.

        \begin{figure}[htp]
             \centering
             \subfile{fig/crossbar_trick}
             \caption{Creation of the indexation-addition operation. I) Identity convolutions are applied to each input. Grey-out parameters can be removed; the light-grey filters would be removed by DCO, but we keep them to for the next step (because the same filter in the other layer is not pruned). Dotted arrows represent the empty feature maps. II) The shrunken convolutions now are able to insert each of the two inputs into larger tensors that can be properly summed together. III) The indices $\mathbf{i}^a$ and $\mathbf{i}^b$ can be directly deduced from the reduced convolutions from step II.}
             \label{fig:crossbar_trick}
         \end{figure}

We developed a method to automatically identify all dependencies, remove disconnected parameters, and adapt operators so that unconstrained filter-pruned networks can be leveraged on embedded devices. This method is generic, tested on various network types, implemented in PyTorch, and freely available (\url{https://github.com/HugoTessier-lab/Neural-Network-Shrinking}). Pruned networks can also be converted to ONNX for multi-hardware deployment.

To reliably identify disconnected weights while avoiding issues such as orphan biases and complex non-linear interactions, we introduced \textit{Dimensional Clear-Out} (DCO). DCO operates on a modified copy of the network in which biases and non-linearities are removed and weights replaced by pruning masks. In this linearized network, any weight with a zero gradient is structurally disconnected due to pruning, allowing identification of all pruned parameters with a single input.

Residual block additions still pose a challenge when tensors have different channel dimensions. To address this, we define an \textit{indexation-addition} operation, which sums only the matching channels from two tensors using configurable index lists. Identity $1\times1$ convolutions are inserted before each addition to automatically generate these indices from the DCO masks. Figure~\ref{fig:crossbar_trick} illustrates this crossbar trick.

In combination, DCO, indexation-addition, and automatic index generation enable any distribution of unconstrained filter pruning to be functional, guaranteeing consistent input/output dimensions and correct residual additions. This framework provides a practical way to study and deploy aggressively pruned networks on hardware.



        The existence of dependencies between layers and the necessity of acknowledging them for a reliable measurement of the compression rate is something that is often left ambiguous in many papers. Blalock \textit{et al.}~\cite{blalock2020state} even cited this lack of consistency, in the metrics reported to characterize the compression of a pruned network, among a large proportion of papers in the field.

        \begin{itemize}
            \item A very naive method, that only counts the proportion of pruned filters.
            \item A more relevant method, that counts the remaining parameters after pruning, but without taking into account any dependencies between networks.
            \item The actual proportion of remaining parameters after DCO.
        \end{itemize}

These experiments did not use LR-rewinding~\cite{renda2020comparing} and networks post-DCO were not fine-tuned, so some of them encountered a small drop in accuracy because of the perturbation of the distribution of data in intermediate representations, due to the removal of orphan biases. \paragraph{Evaluation of structured pruning on GPU}

This contribution~\cite{tessier:hal-03767636} focuses on a detailed analysis of the energy consumption of HRNet segmentation networks compressed by filter pruning using DCO, compared to smaller non-pruned networks (HRNet-18 and HRNet-32). The goal was to assess to what extent pruning effectively reduces energy consumption, and to identify the methodological and practical limitations of this approach.

The results highlight that energy consumption is primarily correlated with the number of operations rather than the number of parameters. Two pruned networks with the same number of parameters can exhibit significantly different energy profiles, since the distribution of sparsity directly influences the density of operations. Consequently, the pruning methods tested (Slimming~\cite{liu2017learning}, SWD~\cite{tessier:hal-03675138}, LR-Rewinding~\cite{renda2020comparing}) yield architectures that do not all lie on the same efficiency–energy trade-off curve, even when they achieve similar compression rates. This finding underlines the complexity of the relationship between pruning criteria, the distribution of retained weights, and the final energy efficiency.

Another key observation is that smaller non-pruned networks often appear more energy-efficient than pruned versions of HRNet-48 with a comparable number of parameters or operations. This is largely due to the overhead introduced by indexation-addition operations, and in particular by the \textit{ScatterND} operator. Although conceptually simple, this operator is poorly optimized in TensorRT: it is treated as a ``Foreign Node,'' executed by Myelin (cuBLAS) rather than cuDNN, leading to disproportionately high latency relative to its theoretical complexity. As a result, even when the number of convolution operations decreases, the overall latency of pruned networks remains higher than that of smaller compact models.

These results lead to several general conclusions. First, the benefits of pruning in terms of energy efficiency are not guaranteed: when compared to native lightweight architectures, compressing a large model does not necessarily bring an advantage. Second, the actual efficiency of a network depends not only on its theoretical architecture but also on the implementation of its operators within inference frameworks. An operator that seems optimal in theory may in practice become a bottleneck if not efficiently supported by the runtime library. Finally, the choice of pruning criterion and method plays a central role, as they determine not only the accuracy (mIoU) but also the distribution of sparsity, which directly affects the number of operations and, consequently, energy consumption.

\subsection{A practical compression case: GoodFloow}


In the era of Industry 4.0, efficient management and tracking of industrial packages has become a key concern for many companies. GoodFloow, a company specializing in sustainable logistics solutions, focuses on replacing disposable cardboard packaging with Reusable Plastic Crates (RPCs) to reduce environmental impact and enhance operational efficiency. Ensuring the real-time monitoring of these packages is essential to prevent loss, damage, or theft, and to guarantee accountability throughout the supply chain.

Traditional methods for tracking packages, such as GPS, Time Difference of Arrival (TDoA), or Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), suffer from limitations in industrial environments. GPS loses accuracy indoors, TDoA requires dense IoT deployments with high interference risk, and BLE-based solutions are constrained to short ranges and costly infrastructure. These limitations make continuous, reliable monitoring difficult, particularly when packages move across multiple sites or logistics partners. Human-based monitoring is an alternative but is both time-consuming and expensive. These challenges highlight the need for IoT systems capable of detecting critical events in a package's lifecycle rather than continuously tracking its exact location.

To address this need, our study leverages accelerometer data combined with deep learning techniques to detect key events, such as a package being handled by a forklift or transported in a truck. By identifying state changes from sensor data, the system can selectively trigger communication or processing, optimizing energy consumption and extending the lifetime of battery-powered IoT devices. Deploying such models directly on constrained hardware introduces additional challenges due to limited memory and computational capabilities. Tiny Machine Learning (TinyML) provides a solution by enabling low-power, low-latency, and secure inference on embedded devices, eliminating the need for continuous data transfer to servers. TinyML has already shown promise in various domains, including agriculture, healthcare, and industrial automation, but its application to package monitoring remains largely unexplored.

The contributions of this work are twofold. First, we demonstrate that embedded deep learning can accurately detect package handling events in an industrial context using accelerometer signals. Second, we provide a methodology that balances high prediction precision and recall with low energy consumption, ensuring IoT devices can remain in sleep mode for extended periods while responding efficiently to relevant events. This methodology is not only applicable to the GoodFloow use case but can also be generalized to other embedded systems dealing with imbalanced time-series data.

Specifically, our approach includes three main aspects: data augmentation adapted to time-coherent, imbalanced datasets; the design of a convolutional neural network (CNN) with a threshold-based prediction mechanism to enhance robustness and reduce unnecessary processing; and efficient model compression to deploy the CNN on ESP32 microcontrollers commonly used in IoT devices. Experimental validation demonstrates that our system can reliably differentiate between key package events while maintaining low energy usage, illustrating the practical feasibility of embedded deep learning for industrial monitoring applications.

\paragraph{Thresholding soft decisions}

Battery lifespan is a critical concern in embedded devices, which have inherently limited energy capacity. In this context, defining a classification threshold for neural network predictions is a key strategy to optimize both performance and energy consumption. By selecting a threshold, the system can prioritize precision over recall, ensuring that predictions are highly reliable even in the presence of imbalanced datasets. Low-confidence predictions that do not surpass the threshold are automatically assigned to a "Dummy" class, which can subsequently trigger energy-efficient verification or correction methods.

The choice of the threshold is guided by the trade-off between precision and recall for each class, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:data_augmentation}. A higher threshold increases prediction precision, minimizing misclassifications that would otherwise require additional computational operations and thus consume more energy. Conversely, a lower threshold would classify more samples directly but at the cost of reduced confidence. For the use case considered here, it is crucial that predictions are correct when made, even if this allows some instances to fall into the "Dummy" class, as errors would otherwise lead to expensive reprocessing.

Threshold values are therefore determined based on model performance metrics, balancing the need for high precision with acceptable recall levels for each class. These thresholds can also be adapted depending on the configuration being tested or the class-specific characteristics of the dataset. By applying this thresholding approach, the embedded system is able to maintain high predictive reliability while reducing unnecessary computation, thereby extending the operational lifetime of the IoT device.

\paragraph{Data augmentation for time series}

As mentioned earlier, we faced in this project the issue of imbalanced datasets that we tackled with data augmentation.

\subsubsection*{Data Augmentation for Imbalanced Time Series}

Imbalanced datasets are common in time-series applications, as data collection often faces practical constraints. These imbalances can lead to misclassifications because the model has fewer examples from minority classes to learn from. A common solution is dataset re-sampling, which increases the representation of minority classes. In this study, we test two interpolation-based augmentation techniques: the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE)~\cite{chawla_smote_2002} and the ADAptive SYNthetic sampling method (ADASYN)~\cite{adasyn}.

For the SMOTE algorithm, we adapt it to preserve temporal continuity in accelerometer data. A new synthetic vector \(x_{new}\) is generated by randomly selecting a sample from the minority class, \(x_{selected}\), identifying its nearest neighbors using Euclidean distance, and linearly interpolating with a randomly chosen neighbor \(x_{neighbor}\) using a coefficient \(\alpha \in [0,1]\):
\[
x_{new} = x_{selected} + \alpha \cdot (x_{neighbor} - x_{selected})
\]
This process is repeated to generate the desired number of synthetic samples, ensuring that the augmented data maintain realistic temporal structure.

ADASYN differs from SMOTE by adaptively determining how many samples to generate for each minority instance, focusing on examples that are close to the decision boundary and harder to classify. For each minority sample, a ratio \(r_i\) is computed based on the number of majority-class neighbors among the \(K\) nearest neighbors:
\[
r_i = \frac{\Delta}{K}
\]
This ratio is normalized to form a density distribution \(\hat{r_i}\), and the number of synthetic samples \(g_i\) generated for each instance is given by:
\[
g_i = \hat{r_i} \cdot G \quad \text{with} \quad G = (m_l - m_s) \cdot \beta
\]
where \(m_l\) and \(m_s\) are the sizes of the majority and minority classes, respectively, and \(\beta \in [0,1]\) is a coefficient controlling the balance level. In our experiments, we use \(K=5\) nearest neighbors and set \(\beta=0.4\) for ADASYN. For fairness, the number of synthetic samples generated by SMOTE was matched to the total generated by ADASYN.

Comparing the two approaches, ADASYN demonstrates superior performance. This is because it concentrates synthetic samples near class boundaries, enhancing the model's ability to distinguish challenging cases, whereas SMOTE distributes samples uniformly across the feature space. When using the selected classification threshold, the model trained with ADASYN achieves a precision of 98.05\% for Forklift and 95.75\% for Truck events. Consequently, ADASYN was selected for subsequent implementations.

Figure~\ref{fig:data_augmentation} illustrates the effect of varying classification thresholds on precision and recall for each data augmentation technique. The curves clearly show that thresholding combined with ADASYN yields high precision while maintaining adequate recall, supporting both robust classification and energy-efficient embedded deployment.

\paragraph{Model compression and embedded deployment}
To deploy the CNN on resource-constrained IoT devices, we apply deep compression techniques combining weight pruning, quantization, and Huffman coding. Using the weight rewinding technique, the model is first trained with all its weights, and then after pruning, unpruned weights are fine-tuned to recover performance. A pruning ratio of 50\% was selected as an optimal trade-off between model size reduction and accuracy~\cite{younes:hal-03850858}. Pruning is applied to convolutional and linear layers to reduce the non-volatile memory footprint, which is often a limiting factor in embedded environments. Huffman coding is then applied to further compress the TFLite model, while dynamic quantization reduces the model size to 14~kB, achieving a compression factor of over four compared to the original model. Importantly, this compression preserves high precision and recall for both Forklift and Truck events, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:deep_compression_results}.

For deployment, the compressed and quantized TFLite model is converted into a C++ source file, which is then flashed onto the ESP32 microcontroller using the Espressif IoT Development Framework (ESP-IDF). Although the ESP32 module has 16~MB of flash memory, it is typically shared with other modules such as WiFi and Bluetooth. The small size of the compressed model allows it to be fully loaded into SRAM, enabling faster access and minimizing memory latency during inference.

Reducing energy consumption is critical to extend the operational lifespan of the IoT device. During typical usage, the device wakes only when motion is detected and performs an inference to determine a state change. The pruned and quantized model consumes approximately 316~mW during inference, with an execution time of 27~ms, while the baseline power consumption of the device is 300~mW when idle. This incremental energy cost is minimal, ensuring that frequent inferences have a limited impact on battery life. The combination of efficient model compression and fast, selective inference allows the IoT device to operate sustainably over extended periods.

\subsection{CNN acceleration with SIMD on ARM and x86 architectures}

I worked through a research contract in 2021-22 with the canadian startup DeepLite, which specializes in optimizing deep learning models for edge devices. My role involved developing techniques to accelerate convolutional neural networks (CNNs) using Single Instruction Multiple Data (SIMD) instructions on ARM and x86 architectures. The core idea was to use SIMD registers as look-up tables (LUTs) to replace computationally intensive operations with simple memory accesses, thereby speeding up inference. This was targetting low-bit quantized models (e.g., INT2, INT4) where the number of possible values is small enough to fit in SIMD registers.

Deep learning models achieve high accuracy but often require a lot of memory and computing power, which makes it difficult to run them efficiently on regular CPUs, especially on small devices. One way to reduce memory and computation is to use ultra low-bit numbers, such as 2-bit values, for storing weights and activations. However, most CPUs are optimized for 8-bit or larger operations, so directly computing with sub-byte numbers is inefficient.

To overcome this limitation, we propose DeepGEMM, which replaces expensive multiplications with a lookup table (LUT) approach. All possible products of the 2-bit weights and activations are precomputed and stored in a small table. At inference time, the CPU simply retrieves the result from the LUT instead of performing a multiplication. This process is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fp lookup}. The algorithm first packs multiple low-bit values into larger data types, then unpacks them to form indices for the table, and finally sums the retrieved values to compute the layer outputs.

Using this method, DeepGEMM achieves both faster computation and lower memory usage. On multiple convolutional neural networks, end-to-end inference shows speedups of up to 1.68$\times$ compared to optimized 8-bit kernels, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:end-to-end speedups}. While the unpacking step is currently the main bottleneck, future improvements such as better packing schemes and operator fusion are expected to further enhance performance. DeepGEMM is also flexible, supporting different number representations, including signed and non-uniform quantization.

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation on FPGA for Autonomous Vehicles}

Hugo Le Blevec's doctoral work focused on the co-design of deep neural networks and pipelined FPGA architectures for semantic segmentation in autonomous vehicles~\cite{leblevec:hal-04262138,leblevec:hal-04705594}. These systems require low latency and reduced consumption, making classic GPUs less suitable for embedded contexts. FPGAs offer a promising alternative due to their parallel processing and energy efficiency, although they pose challenges related to limited resources. This work is not currently published but is under submission to ACM TRETS.

Over the past decade, the rapid progress of deep learning has been driven not only by algorithmic advances but also by the availability of large datasets and the computing platforms capable of handling them. Our work fits into this trajectory by focusing on the question of how to bring high-performance inference to environments where efficiency, latency, and energy consumption are critical. In this context, FPGAs emerged as a natural candidate: they offer a fine balance between flexibility and efficiency, while enabling custom hardware designs that can be adapted to the specific demands of neural network workloads.

Our research has been anchored in the study of representative datasets that reflect real-world application domains, ranging from image classification to more complex tasks requiring low-latency inference. The choice of these datasets was not incidental: they provided a benchmark against which we could measure the relevance of our contributions, both in terms of raw accuracy and in the broader perspective of system-level efficiency.

The central objective has been to establish new state-of-the-art performance for neural network inference on FPGA platforms. This ambition was made possible thanks to a close collaboration with the FINN team at AMD Dublin, whose toolchain offered both a foundation and a playground for innovative design strategies. Our long-term investment in the FINN ecosystem not only consolidated our methodological framework but also opened doors to industrial collaborations, such as with the company ARKANE, and to participation in larger initiatives like the VORTEX project. These partnerships have illustrated the dual impact of our work: academic recognition through the advancement of methods, and industrial relevance through real-world deployments.

\paragraph{Task and dataset}


The task addressed in this work is \textit{semantic segmentation}, which consists in assigning a class label to every pixel of an image. Compared to classical image classification, where a single label is predicted for the entire image, semantic segmentation provides a fine-grained understanding of the scene and is therefore particularly relevant for autonomous driving, where distinguishing between road, vehicles, pedestrians, and other objects is essential for safety.

Our interest in this task originates from the work of Hugo Le Blevec during his PhD thesis at Stellantis, which first introduced us to the challenges of deploying deep learning for road scene understanding. While image classification was a natural entry point, semantic segmentation appeared both more challenging and more rewarding. It poses a significant implementation difficulty on FPGA devices, as models for semantic segmentation typically rely on non-standard network components (e.g., dilated convolutions, encoder-decoder structures, multi-scale features), and are considerably heavier than classification networks. Achieving competitive accuracy while still fitting the stringent resource and latency constraints of embedded FPGAs thus represents a major research challenge.

The performance of semantic segmentation models is generally measured with the mean Intersection over Union (mIoU). This metric evaluates, for each class, the ratio between the correctly predicted pixels (true positives) and the union of predicted and ground-truth pixels (true positives, false positives, and false negatives). Averaging this score across classes provides a robust measure that accounts for class imbalance and is widely adopted as the main benchmark in the field.

In this work, we focus on two widely used datasets for road scene segmentation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CamVid} (Cambridge-driving Labeled Video Database) contains 701 images extracted from driving video sequences, at an original resolution of $960\times720$ pixels. The dataset defines 11 semantic classes. For efficiency, especially during inference, the images are often downsampled to $480\times360$ pixels, which is the resolution adopted in our experiments. While relatively small, CamVid provides a useful benchmark for comparing approaches and validating embedded implementations.

    \item \textbf{Cityscapes} is a much larger dataset with 5000 finely annotated urban street images, originally at $2048\times1024$ resolution. It defines 30 semantic classes, of which 19 are commonly used for evaluation. Due to its higher resolution and greater class diversity, Cityscapes represents a far more challenging benchmark than CamVid and has become the de facto standard for autonomous driving research. In practice, lower resolutions (e.g., $1024\times512$ or $512\times256$) are often used during inference to reduce memory and computation costs. In this work, we primarily focus on Cityscapes due to its difficulty and relevance, while still reporting results on CamVid for broader comparison.
\end{itemize}

In summary, semantic segmentation of road scenes is both a crucial and demanding task. It provides the right level of difficulty to study FPGA implementations of neural networks, forcing a careful balance between accuracy and hardware efficiency, and motivating the exploration carried out in this work.


\paragraph{Choosing and training a deep learning model for embedded semantic segmentation}

Several lightweight encoder–decoder architectures have been proposed for real-time semantic segmentation. Table~\ref{tab:retrain} lists some of the most representative models and their reported results on Cityscapes, along with the improved results we obtained after retraining. A brief description of each model follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ENet}~\cite{paszke2016enet} (2016): one of the earliest real-time segmentation networks, with only 0.37M parameters. It employs a compact encoder–decoder design with bottleneck modules and dilated convolutions to balance accuracy and efficiency.
    \item \textbf{ESPNet}~\cite{mehta2018espnetefficientspatialpyramid} (2018): designed for efficiency through a spatial pyramid of dilated convolutions, which allows capturing multi-scale context while keeping the parameter count low (0.4M).
    \item \textbf{DABNet}~\cite{li2019dabnetdepthwiseasymmetricbottleneck} (2019): uses depthwise separable and asymmetric convolutions inside bottleneck blocks, yielding a lightweight (0.76M parameters) yet accurate design.
    \item \textbf{EDANet}~\cite{lo2019efficientdensemodulesasymmetric} (2019): an efficient dense architecture that combines dense connections with asymmetric convolutions, reaching good trade-offs at 0.68M parameters.
    \item \textbf{Fast-SCNN}~\cite{poudel2019fastscnnfastsemanticsegmentation} (2019): explicitly designed for embedded systems, it combines a shallow network for downsampling and a lightweight context path with depthwise separable convolutions (1.1M parameters).
    \item \textbf{ESNet}~\cite{wang2019esnetefficientsymmetricnetwork} (2019): builds on an efficient symmetric encoder–decoder structure with channel shuffle operations, leading to competitive results at 1.66M parameters.
\end{itemize}

Despite being the oldest of the group, ENet is by far the most compact model and was long considered obsolete due to its lower accuracy. However, as highlighted in the image classification domain by the ``ResNet Strikes Back'' paper~\cite{wightman2021resnet}, performance comparisons are often biased because training routines differ substantially between studies. When properly retrained with modern techniques, ENet can achieve competitive performance without increasing complexity.

We evaluated this hypothesis through an ablation study of training settings, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}. Starting from the original ENet (58.3\% mIoU), we progressively introduced a longer training schedule, optimized hyperparameters, stronger data augmentation, and revised cropping and resizing strategies. Each factor contributes, but their combination is crucial: only with the full setup do we reach 70.5\% mIoU on Cityscapes. This demonstrates that the training pipeline can have a larger impact on performance than the choice of architecture itself.

\paragraph{Hardware architecture and design space exploration}


We implement ENet as a fully pipelined dataflow architecture, where every layer of the network is mapped to a dedicated hardware block on the FPGA and processed in a streaming manner. This approach, unlike DPU-based sequential execution, avoids external memory transfers and allows much higher throughput, which is crucial under real-time constraints. The design is carried out with the FINN framework~\cite{blott2018finnr, Umuroglu_2017}, which requires adapting the network to comply with HLS limitations. Several modifications were therefore introduced to make ENet compatible with this flow, in particular replacing PReLU activations with ReLUs, reshaping transposed convolutions to avoid output padding, replacing MaxUnpool operations with equivalent ConvTranspose layers, substituting MaxPool+Concat in downsampling branches by strided convolutions, simplifying the InitialBlock, and adjusting the placement of ReLU activations around skip connections. These changes ensure compatibility with FINN and quantization while limiting accuracy degradation.

Quantization-aware training was performed using Brevitas~\cite{brevitas}, with INT4 weights and activations and INT8 in the first and last layers. This choice provides a good trade-off between accuracy and hardware efficiency, significantly reducing resource usage and power consumption while keeping segmentation performance close to the floating-point baseline. Interestingly, some structural modifications even slightly improved accuracy, while the most detrimental was the change in ReLU placement within skip connections. Overall, the adapted network shows less than 1\% drop in mIoU compared to the original model, at the cost of an increase in parameters from 354K to 513K, which remains very small compared to other real-time models.

We deployed the modified ENet on an AMD ZU19EG FPGA, clocked at 300 MHz. Two configurations were implemented: a smaller, power-efficient version ($\text{P-ENet}_S$) and a more parallelized version targeting maximum throughput ($\text{P-ENet}_M$). In addition, a CamVid-trained version ($\text{P-ENet}_C$) was produced. Resource utilization of these designs is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:impl}. None of the designs required DSP blocks, as INT4 arithmetic could be efficiently implemented using LUTs.

Beyond these adaptations, we explored variations of the architecture to optimize the trade-off between accuracy and resource usage. Increasing the projection ratio in bottleneck modules reduces computations but severely harms accuracy, while reintroducing shortcuts between encoder and decoder only marginally improves accuracy at a high resource cost. In contrast, replacing transposed convolutions with nearest-neighbor upsampling proved the most effective modification, reducing computations from 1.47G to 1.11G MACs on Cityscapes while preserving accuracy. After fine-tuning, this model achieved 70.3\% mIoU, less than 0.2\% below the floating-point baseline.

The final implementations demonstrate that lightweight models such as ENet, when properly adapted and quantized, can reach state-of-the-art efficiency on embedded FPGAs. On Cityscapes, $\text{P-ENet}_S$ achieves 126 FPS with 8 ms latency per frame, while $\text{P-ENet}_M$ reaches 226 FPS with 4.4 ms latency. On CamVid, $\text{P-ENet}_C$ achieves 76.3\% mIoU with 171 FPS and 5.8 ms latency. These results highlight that a careful combination of architectural simplification, quantization, and pipelined hardware design allows real-time semantic segmentation within tight resource constraints.

Compared to the UNet implementation of~\cite{Miyama_2021}, our design improves accuracy by over 8\% and throughput by nearly 40\%, while consuming fewer DSPs. These results show that careful retraining, architectural adaptations for compatibility with a pipelined dataflow framework, and quantization-aware design enable ENet to achieve state-of-the-art accuracy and efficiency on FPGAs, highlighting the importance of hardware–algorithm co-design.


\subsection{PEFSL: Few-Shot Learning on FPGA-SoC}

In parallel, the PEFSL (Pipeline for Embedded Few-Shot Learning) project, originating from a student project I supervised with Lucas Grativol, winner of the 2023 OpenHardware AMD Xilinx competition\footnote{https://www.openhw.eu/2023-results-gallery}, tackled few-shot learning on FPGA-SoC. The developed flow, based on Tensil, covers the entire training, design chain, and deployment on a PYNQ-Z1 board. The demonstrator achieves a latency of 30 ms for a consumption of 6.2 W on MiniImageNet~\cite{grativol:hal-04559365}.

\paragraph{Embedded Few Shot Learning}

Conventional object classification relies on training deep neural networks (DNNs) with large labeled datasets, which are often costly to obtain~\cite{fredriksson2020data}. Few-shot learning (FSL) addresses this limitation by enabling robust classification with only a few labeled examples per class, leveraging knowledge from pre-trained networks through transfer learning or fine-tuning~\cite{weiss2016survey,b2}.

Implementing FSL on embedded systems remains challenging due to the computational and memory demands of DNNs. FPGA SoCs have emerged as promising candidates for low-power and low-latency deployment~\cite{b10,b9,zhang2021low}, but literature examples are scarce. Key challenges include adapting training routines, designing lightweight backbones, and integrating deployment frameworks for real-time inference on embedded hardware.

In this paper, we address these challenges with the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item One of the first platforms for real-time few-shot object classification on FPGA SoCs.
    \item An open-source pipeline\footnote{\url{https://github.com/brain-bzh/PEFSL}} based on the Tensil framework\footnote{\url{https://www.tensil.ai/}} for backbone design, training, evaluation, and deployment on FPGA.
    \item Demonstration on the MiniImageNet dataset of a low-power, low-latency few-shot model deployed on FPGA hardware.
\end{itemize}

These contributions enable applications in robotics, drones, and autonomous vehicles where responsiveness and energy efficiency are critical.

Few-shot learning aims to classify examples of unseen classes with only a few labeled samples. State-of-the-art methods rely on DNN backbones~\cite{b2}, trained first on a large base dataset with a pretext loss~\cite{gidaris2019boosting} to extract feature embeddings.

Evaluation occurs on a novel dataset split into episodes. Each episode has a number of \emph{ways} (classes), \emph{shots} (labeled examples per class), and \emph{queries} (unlabeled examples). Model performance is measured by accuracy on queries averaged over episodes~\cite{laenen2021episodes}. Our work focuses on the inductive setting~\cite{scott2018adapted}, where queries are not known beforehand.

Designing efficient backbones is essential for embedded deployment, enabling rapid adaptation to new tasks with limited computational resources, particularly for real-time object recognition in drones and robots.

\paragraph{Design space exploration of the backbone}

Our experiments rely on small ResNet architectures~\cite{he2016deep}, using residual blocks with skip connections to enable deeper and more accurate networks than traditional CNNs~\cite{li2021survey}. We focus on ResNet-9 and ResNet-12, which offer a good trade-off between performance, number of parameters, and computational complexity (Fig.~\ref{resnet9}). Key hyperparameters include network depth, training and test image size, downsampling strategy (strided convolutions or max pooling), and number of feature maps, which together balance accuracy and latency.

We train these networks on MiniImageNet~\cite{vinyals2016matching}, consisting of 64 base, 16 validation, and 20 novel classes with $84\times84$ images, focusing on a 5-way, 1-shot setup. Data augmentation (multiple crops) improves feature generalization for few-shot learning.

A design space exploration was conducted to identify configurations that optimize both accuracy and real-time performance. Networks were compiled with the Tensil framework to estimate inference cycles. For $32\times32$ images, ResNet-9 outperforms ResNet-12, likely because deeper networks produce last-layer feature maps that are too small for effective few-shot classification. Matching training and test image sizes ($32\times32$) yields better results than training on larger images. Strided convolutions reduce latency without impacting accuracy, and adjusting the number of feature maps allows tuning the trade-off between speed and performance (Fig.~\ref{result_implem}).

For our embedded application on the PYNQ-Z1, prioritizing real-time inference, the optimal configuration is a strided ResNet-9 with 16 feature maps, trained and tested on $32\times32$ images, providing an excellent compromise between latency and accuracy.

\section{New Methods and Architectures for Channel Codes}
\subsection{The Agentic Paradigm in Channel Coding Research}

The development and maintenance of large-scale open-source scientific software, such as AFF3CT, successfully aggregated the knowledge of a community but faced a structural bottleneck inherent to academic research: the scarcity of human resources. Unlike industrial tech giants, academic labs operate with a limited number of permanent staff and a turnover of PhD students. Maintaining a highly optimized, vectorized, and multi-threaded C++ codebase required a level of expertise and time investment that often competed with the primary goal of publishing new research. This resource constraint limited the pace of innovation and the ability to maintain a "production-grade" infrastructure over time.

However, the recent emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) and autonomous software engineering agents significantly shifts this paradigm. We are moving from a model where the limiting factor was the sustainable human effort to \textit{produce} and \textit{maintain} code, to a model where agents can act as force multipliers. In this new era, legacy open-source projects like AFF3CT gain a strategic value: they provide the robust, automated verification environment—or "Ground Truth"—that enables agents to work safely and effectively.

\subsection{AFF3CT in the Era of Autonomous Software Engineering}

The rise of AI software engineers offers an unprecedented opportunity to revitalize scientific libraries by handling the "heavy lifting" of software engineering. Beyond simply writing algorithms, agents can address the most tedious aspects of our CI/CD pipelines which previously consumed disproportionate human time.
Specifically, implementing and maintaining rigorous test cases in AFF3CT is a task requiring high domain expertise. It is not sufficient to simply "run simulations"; one must ensure the scientific relevance of the parameters (e.g., adhering to 3GPP or DVB-S2 standards), foresee pathological cases, and maintain strict consistency across architectures. We envision an agentic workflow where these critical maintenance tasks are delegated to autonomous systems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Automated Test Curation and Strategy}: Beyond simple load balancing, agents can actively scan the state of the art and standardization documents to define the most relevant test scenarios. They can identify "gold standard" configurations (e.g., specific code rates $R=1/2$ or $R=5/6$ used in benchmarks) and prioritize test cases that reveal known weaknesses, such as error floors or specific trapping sets. This moves the test design from a manual, ad-hoc process to a systematic, literature-driven exploration.
    \item \textbf{Robust Reference Generation}: Generating "Golden Reference" files requires covering a massive combinatorial space of simulation parameters (channel model, modulation, code family, rate, blocked length, decoding algorithm, etc.). Agents can do more than passively maintain this database; they can make \textit{rational decisions} on which simulations to execute via the Command Line Interface (CLI). By prioritizing scenarios that maximize information gain or regression coverage within a fixed computational budget, they effectively orchestrate the Continuous Integration pipeline.
    \item \textbf{Verification as the Bottleneck}: With agents capable of generating C++ implementations of new algorithms (like the GRM-SCF~\cite{Sagitov2025}), the role of the human researcher shifts to defining the \textit{validation criteria}. The AFF3CT infrastructure becomes the automated "Oracle". The agent doesn't just write the code; it designs the adversarial validation harness intended to break it, ensuring that innovations are robust before they ever reach human review.
\end{itemize}

A concrete application is the implementation of the \textit{Generalized Restart Mechanism} (GRM)~\cite{Sagitov2025}. Instead of a PhD student spending months ensuring memory alignment and vectorization for a complex restart-based decoder, an agent could extract the logic from the paper, generate the C++ module, and—crucially—use the agentic-enhanced AFF3CT harness to verify it against thousands of test vectors, ensuring it meets the strict latency and accuracy requirements before a human ever reviews the code.

\subsection{Hardware Exploration Facilitated by Evolutionary Algorithms}

\subsubsection{State of the Art and Limitations}
The customization of RISC-V architectures for domain-specific applications is currently a thriving field. Industrial tools like Andes \textit{ACE}~\cite{AndesACE} or academic frameworks like \textit{CIDRE}~\cite{CIDRE2024} and \textit{MARVEL}~\cite{MARVEL2024} allow designers to map existing hot-spots of C code to custom instructions. These tools excel at automating the \textit{implementation} phase: generating the RTL and toolchain support for instructions defined by the user. However, they remain limited by human intuition: they optimize what the engineer explicitly expresses in the software algorithm. They do not \textit{invent} new algorithmic primitives or discover non-intuitive bit-level manipulations that could drastically reduce the critical path of a decoder. The next leap in performance requires shifting from automated implementation to automated \textit{invention}.

\subsubsection{Methodology: The Evolutionary Exploration Loop}
Inspired by DeepMind's \textit{AlphaEvolve}~\cite{AlphaEvolve2025}, which utilizes evolutionary strategies to discover novel learning algorithms, I propose to apply a "genetic exploration" methodology to the design of Domain-Specific Architectures (DSA) for channel coding. This approach closes the loop between an LLM-based generative agent and the rigorous simulation environment of AFF3CT:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genotype (Proposal)}: An LLM acts as the mutation operator. It takes a baseline decoding kernel (e.g., a Polar SC node update) and proposes variations of RISC-V custom instructions, represented as behavioral C++ models or Domain Specific Language (DSL) snippets.
    \item \textbf{Phenotype (Simulation)}: The AFF3CT simulator, instrumented to emulate these custom instructions at the cycle level, executes the full decoding chain.
    \item \textbf{Fitness Function (Evaluation)}: The simulator returns a composite score reflecting Decoding Throughput (Mbps), Latency (cycles), and an area estimate (gate equivalent).
    \item \textbf{Evolution}: This feedback is fed back to the agent, which iterates to refined instruction candidates, effectively navigating the non-convex design space of hardware acceleration.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Risks and Challenges}
This ambitious approach entails significant scientific risks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Verification Explosion}: Validating the correctness of randomly evolved hardware instructions is critical. An instruction might speed up nominal decoding but fail in corner cases (e.g., specific noise patterns or overflows). AFF3CT's role as a "Ground Truth" oracle is paramount here, but formal verification methods might also be needed.
    \item \textbf{Physical Feasibility}: An LLM might propose an instruction that is logically correct but physically unimplementable within a reasonable clock cycle (creating a prohibitively long critical path). The fitness function must therefore rely on accurate hardware cost models, potentially involving synthesis-in-the-loop.
    \item \textbf{Convergence}: Evolutionary search in such a vast space can easily get stuck in local optima. Balancing exploration (trying radically new instructions) and exploitation (refining existing ones) will be a key algorithmic challenge.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Long Term Goal}
The ultimate vision is effectively a "Self-Optimizing Modem" for 6G. We aim to demonstrate a system where, upon the release of a new coding standard or a change in channel conditions, the system can autonomously explore and synthesize the optimal hardware/software partition, updating its own RISC-V micro-architecture (on an FPGA fabric) to deliver maximum energy efficiency.

\section{Network Specialization for Embedded Targets}

As previously shown, numerous contributions have been made in the field of deep learning on embedded targets, particularly concerning model compression and their deployment on specific hardware architectures. This work motivates new developments. On the one hand, their application in specific domains, notably their use in the context of signal jamming in launchers or the processing of EEG signals. On the other hand, the need to take a step back and find more systematic approaches for designing deep learning models adapted to the constraints of embedded targets, particularly by using co-design techniques between models and hardware architectures based on NAS (Neural Architecture Search).

\subsection{Application of Deep Learning for GNSS Denoising in a Spatial Context}

\subsubsection{Context: The Anti-Jamming Challenge}
Following several works carried out within the framework of research contracts with the company Arkane, a central issue emerged: the persistent vulnerability of Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) to interference and intentional jamming. This vulnerability is critical for autonomous systems, specifically in space environments (launchers, satellites) where signal integrity ensures mission success.

\subsubsection{State of the Art: The Complexity of Matrix Inversion}
The project focuses on \textbf{Controlled Reception Pattern Antennas (CRPA)}, which use an antenna array to spatially filter the signal. The classical gold standard for such processing is \textbf{Space-Time Adaptive Processing (STAP)}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism}: STAP involves estimating the covariance matrix of the incoming noise and inverting it to compute optimal beamforming weights.
    \item \textbf{FPGA Implementation Challenges}: Matrix inversion is computationally expensive ($O(N^3)$ complexity). State-of-the-art FPGA implementations, such as the work by \textbf{Zhang et al.}~\cite{Zhang2021}, employ advanced techniques like \textbf{LDL factorization based on Cholesky decomposition} and deeply pipelined triangular matrix inversion to achieve high throughput.
    \item \textbf{Limitation}: Despite these optimizations, the resource consumption (DSP slices) scales rapidly with the antenna array size ($N$). For highly dynamic environments (e.g., spinning launchers) requiring ultra-low latency updates, even optimized matrix inversions become prohibitive on constrained avionics FPGAs.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Methodology: Deep Learning as an Alternative}
To overcome these limitations, the proposed Ph.D. work (Elise Lagarde) explores the integration of Deep Learning to replace or augment the classical processing chain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Trend}: Recent literature confirms the potential of Deep Learning for interference mitigation. Models like \textbf{LcxNet}~\cite{LcxNet2024} or LSTM-based approaches~\cite{Munina2020} demonstrate robust detection and classification of jamming signatures.
    \item \textbf{Algorithmic Shift}: We propose to replace the iterative matrix inversion with a \textbf{quantized CNN}. The network learns the mapping between antenna inputs and optimal weights (or clean signal), transforming an optimization problem ($O(N^3)$) into a fixed-latency inference problem ($O(1)$).
    \item \textbf{FPGA Co-design}: Unlike generic software models, our focus is on \textbf{Hardware-Aware Training}. We constrain the neural network design (power-of-two quantization, structured pruning) specifically to fit within the DSP limits of radiation-hardened FPGAs, bridging the gap between academic DL models and space-grade hardware.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Risks and Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generalization to Unknown Jammers}: A fundamental risk is the behavior of the neural network when facing a jamming signature widely different from its training set. Unlike analytical STAP, which relies on physics, a CNN might hallucinate a solution outside its distribution.
    \item \textbf{Data Scarcity}: Real-world jammed GNSS data is rare and often classified. We must rely heavily on high-fidelity simulators, introducing a "Sim-to-Real" gap that must be bridged.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Long Term Goal}
The ultimate objective is to produce a hardened, autonomous anti-jamming IP block for space launchers. This module would be capable of self-reconfiguration to adapt to evolving threat landscapes, directly contributing to the sovereignty of navigation solutions.

\subsection{Attention Layers on Microcontroller Targets for EEG Data Processing}

A project currently being structured within the BRAIN team focuses on the application of Transformers to real-time processing of electroencephalographic (EEG) signals in embedded environments. Transformer-type architectures have recently shown very good performance for analyzing EEG data, particularly due to their ability to capture long-range dependencies in multichannel signals~\cite{tegon2025}. This methodological orientation is increasingly present in the literature but remains little explored in the context of embedded deployment.

A notable specificity of EEG work is the use of long time sequences, often necessary to capture brain rhythms or discrete events. This poses a major challenge for embedded systems, as the complexity of Transformers grows rapidly with sequence length. In contrast, unlike what is observed in language or image processing, EEG models use relatively compact architectures, which makes their execution on microcontrollers feasible, provided targeted optimization work.

The project's objective is to design a real-time embedded processing demonstrator based on Transformers optimized for EEG. This involves rigorous hardware-software co-design: memory footprint control, operation vectorization to take advantage of microcontroller parallel architectures, and adaptation of model layers to hardware constraints. A collaboration is planned with Giulia Lioi, to ensure the relevance of the methods and tasks envisioned and to anchor the project in concrete use cases. This work could lead to lightweight, autonomous devices capable of localized EEG processing, with potential applications in neurotechnology, portable diagnostics, or brain-machine interfaces.

\subsection{Co-design of Neural Networks and Embedded Hardware Architectures}

Finally, a possible improvement to my current work lies in the fact that architecture design is performed manually. Neural Architecture Search (NAS) is a well-known and frequently cited approach for automating the design of neural network architectures. However, this field is often perceived as disconnected from practical realities, producing architectures that are not always optimized for real-world constraints.

Zero-cost proxies (ZCPs), which enable low-cost NAS by avoiding full model training, present a fundamental limitation~\cite{lymanson:hal-04576139}. Although they show a strong correlation with model performance across the entire search space, this correlation drastically decreases when focusing on the best architectures. ZCPs tend to be biased towards architectures with a large number of parameters, which reduces their discriminatory power for high-performance models. This weakness is particularly notable when the search space is reduced to the top 1\% of architectures, where the correlation between ZCPs and actual performance drops significantly.

To overcome this problem, my objective is to develop more pragmatic techniques to automatically design lightweight neural network architectures, whether Transformers or CNNs. Preliminary work in this direction has already been carried out. We are currently focusing on sampling techniques on supernets, guided by zero-shot metrics, to automate this design more efficiently and adapted to implementation constraints. The number of model parameters is considered a simple but universal discriminant metric to constrain the search space, thus targeting regions more suitable for ZCP-guided exploration. This approach aims to improve the quality of discovered architectures, especially for ZCPs that struggle to find high-performing regions of the space on their own, and allows controlling the size of sampled models, thus avoiding over-parameterizing architectures.

Similarly, an effective technique for designing neural network models by NAS is the Supernet paradigm. This involves training a neural network model encompassing a wide variety of possible architectures, thereby enabling the discovery of high-performing models without having to train each architecture individually. One of the problems is how to perform the training so that, in the end, sub-networks are capable of functioning autonomously. In most methods, a selection of sub-networks to be agglomerated with the loss is performed, but this selection is done randomly. We could instead focus on architectures that are known to be high-performing, for example by using zero-cost proxies. Or, by using expert models on the suitability of sub-networks for given hardware architectures. The Stream tool developed by the MICAS team at the University of Louvain allows for this modeling, for example~\cite{symons2022}. We have contacted this team, and Jérémy Morlier, a doctoral student as part of my JCJC ProPruNN project, has begun working on integrating Stream into the search for sub-networks for specific hardware architectures. A mobility period in autumn 2025 is planned to deepen this collaboration and explore the possibilities offered by Stream in the context of searching for sub-networks adapted to hardware constraints.
